CHAPTER ONE
INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background and Statement of the Problem
The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and mobile computing technologies has opened new possibilities for transforming healthcare delivery, particularly in
regions where access to skilled medical personnel and diagnostic infrastructure remains
severely limited. In low-resource settings across sub-Saharan Africa, healthcare systems
continue to face persistent challenges, including inadequate facilities, insufficient diagnostic equipment, and a critical shortage of trained medical professionals capable of interpreting complex clinical data World Health Organisation (2022). These challenges collectively undermine the quality, timeliness, and accuracy of disease diagnosis and patient
management, contributing significantly to preventable morbidity and mortality across the
region.
Cameroon operates a three-tiered health system comprising central, intermediate, and
peripheral levels, with the peripheral level serving as the primary point of contact for the
majority of the population. At this level, health centres are typically headed by stateregistered nurses, whilst district hospitals are managed by general practitioners who are
expected to diagnose and treat a wide range of conditions with limited specialist support Mougang et al. (2020). The burden placed on this workforce is considerable, with a
doctor-to-patient ratio of approximately 1:15,939 and a nurse-to-patient ratio of 1:4,260
nationally Mougang et al. (2020). These figures reflect a healthcare system under significant strain, where clinical decisions are routinely made without access to specialist
diagnostic expertise.
The situation is further compounded in the North-West and South-West regions of
Cameroon by an ongoing socio-political crisis that began in 2016 and escalated into
armed conflict in 2017. This crisis, widely referred to as the Anglophone crisis, has
resulted in the destruction or abandonment of more than 210 health facilities across the
two regions United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (2023).
From January to July 2023 alone, at least 16 incidents of violence against or obstruction of healthcare were reported in the North-West and South-West regions, including
damage to health infrastructure, looting of medical equipment, and threats against health-

1

care workers United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (2023).
Health personnel have resigned in large numbers for fear of reprisals, further depleting
an already overstretched workforce and leaving communities in the North-West Region,
including Bamenda, with severely diminished access to basic healthcare services Fonkwo
et al. (2023).
Within this context, infectious diseases such as malaria and tuberculosis represent the
most significant and persistent diagnostic challenges at the peripheral healthcare level.
Malaria is the most widespread endemic disease in Cameroon, annually responsible for
more than two million reported cases and accounting for approximately 3% of all global
malaria cases Severe Malaria Observatory (2023). The disease accounts for 50% of all
hospitalisations in health facilities nationally, with 65% of cases occurring among children under five years of age Severe Malaria Observatory (2023). Diagnosis traditionally
relies on microscopic examination of blood smear slides by trained laboratory personnel, a process that demands skilled technicians and well-maintained equipment that many
peripheral health facilities are consistently unable to provide Liang et al. (2016). Tuberculosis similarly represents a critical public health burden, with an estimated incidence
rate of 164 cases per 100,000 persons in Cameroon and a mortality rate exceeding 28%
Nkwescheu et al. (2023). Accurate TB diagnosis requires interpretation of chest X-ray
images by trained radiologists, a cadre of specialists largely concentrated in urban referral hospitals and largely unavailable at the district and health centre levels where most
patients first present World Health Organisation (2023a).
Compounding these diagnostic challenges is the continued reliance on paper-based
patient record systems across the majority of health facilities in Cameroon. Physical
consultation booklets are frequently lost, damaged, or unavailable during clinical encounters, resulting in fragmented patient histories and uninformed clinical decisions. The
absence of a structured digital patient management infrastructure further limits the ability of healthcare providers to track disease progression, monitor treatment outcomes, and
support continuity of care across facilities.
Recent advances in deep learning, a subset of artificial intelligence, have demonstrated
considerable potential in addressing these gaps. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
have achieved diagnostic accuracy comparable to expert clinicians in tasks such as the detection of pulmonary tuberculosis from chest X-ray images and the identification of mal-

2

aria parasites from blood smear microscopy Rajpurkar et al. (2017); Liang et al. (2016).
However, the majority of existing AI-based diagnostic systems were developed and validated using high-quality datasets from well-resourced clinical environments, rendering
them ill-suited for deployment in settings where imaging conditions are variable, data
is frequently incomplete, and internet connectivity is unreliable Obermeyer et al. (2019).
Furthermore, most existing systems address single diagnostic tasks in isolation and do not
integrate patient management, clinical communication, and offline operational capability
within a single accessible platform.
This study therefore addressed these gaps by designing and developing a mobile artificial intelligence system for clinical diagnosis support and patient management in lowresource healthcare settings. The system integrated deep learning models for malaria
and tuberculosis detection, a digital patient record platform, and an AI-assisted clinical
chat feature within a hybrid offline/online mobile application, providing a comprehensive
and contextually appropriate tool for healthcare delivery in resource-constrained environments such as Cameroon.
1.2 Rationale
The rationale for this study is grounded in three converging realities: the critical shortage of diagnostic expertise in Cameroonian healthcare facilities, the demonstrated potential of artificial intelligence in medical image analysis, and the growing penetration of
mobile technology across sub-Saharan Africa as a viable platform for healthcare service
delivery.
As established in Section 1.1, healthcare facilities at the peripheral level in Cameroon
routinely operate without access to trained radiologists or laboratory specialists capable
of interpreting diagnostic imaging for diseases such as tuberculosis and malaria. The
consequences of this shortage are measurable and severe, contributing directly to delayed
diagnosis, inappropriate treatment, and avoidable deaths. Existing AI-based diagnostic
tools have demonstrated the technical capacity to address this gap; however, the majority
were developed using high-quality datasets from well-resourced environments and have
not been designed to function reliably under the conditions that characterise Cameroonian healthcare settings, including degraded imaging equipment, variable image quality,
and unreliable internet connectivity Sendra-Balcells et al. (2023). A systematic scoping

3

review on deploying medical AI in low-resource settings identified unreliable internet access, incomplete data, and limited familiarity with AI among healthcare workers as the
most common barriers to effective deployment Al-Ganad et al. (2026). These barriers are
directly applicable to the Cameroonian context and informed the design decisions made
in this study.
The choice to focus on malaria and tuberculosis as the target diseases was driven by
their status as the two most diagnostically underserved high-burden infectious diseases in
Cameroon. Both diseases are amenable to image-based AI diagnosis, with malaria detectable from blood smear microscopy images and tuberculosis identifiable from chest X-ray
images, making them well-suited to a unified deep learning pipeline within a single mobile platform. Furthermore, both diseases disproportionately affect the communities most
distant from specialist diagnostic services, reinforcing the appropriateness of a mobilebased solution designed for peripheral healthcare delivery.
The rationale for adopting a mobile-first architecture is supported by evidence of rapid
and sustained growth in mobile technology adoption across sub-Saharan Africa. According to the Global System for Mobile Communications Association, smartphones accounted for 49% of mobile connections in sub-Saharan Africa in 2022 and were projected
to account for 61% of connections by 2025 Gahagan et al. (2023). Mobile health applications have been shown to improve patient monitoring, medication adherence, and
healthcare worker communication, particularly in rural areas where fixed infrastructure
is limited Betjeman et al. (2013). The widespread availability of smartphones among
healthcare workers in Cameroon therefore presents a practical and scalable platform for
deploying AI-assisted diagnostic tools without requiring the installation of specialised
hardware.
The decision to incorporate a hybrid offline and online architecture further strengthens
the rationale for this study. Connectivity remains inconsistent across much of Cameroon,
particularly in rural areas in Cameroon. A system designed to function exclusively over
an internet connection would be fundamentally unsuitable for this environment. By enabling on-device inference using compressed deep learning models, the proposed system
ensured that diagnostic support remained available to healthcare workers regardless of
network availability, with results synchronised to the central server when connectivity
was restored.

a longitudinal digital health record accessible to both doctors and patients, this study addressed a broader and more realistic picture of what is required to improve healthcare

1.3 Research Questions
1.3.1 Main Research Question
How can a mobile artificial intelligence system be designed and developed to support
clinical diagnosis and patient management in low-resource healthcare settings?
1.3.2 Specific Research Questions
The following specific research questions guided the study:
1. How can a convolutional neural network be trained to accurately detect tuberculosis
from chest X-ray images that are subject to real-world degradation and variability
in low-resource clinical environments?
2. How can a convolutional neural network be trained to accurately detect malaria
from blood smear images captured using a mobile phone camera through a microscope eyepiece, accounting for domain shift between training data and real-world
acquisition conditions?
3. How can deep learning models for medical image classification be optimised for
deployment on mobile devices with limited computational resources and unreliable
internet connectivity?
4. How can a mobile-based platform be designed to integrate AI-assisted diagnosis,
digital patient records, and a patient-aware clinical chat feature within a secure and
accessible application for use in low-resource healthcare settings?

LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Introduction
This chapter presents a systematic review of existing literature relevant to the design
and development of a mobile artificial intelligence system for clinical diagnosis support
and patient management in low-resource healthcare settings. The review is organised
thematically, covering the key conceptual and technical areas that underpin the present
study. These areas include the application of artificial intelligence in healthcare, deep
learning for medical image analysis, convolutional neural network-based approaches for
tuberculosis and malaria detection, methods for handling degraded and noisy medical
images, mobile artificial intelligence and model optimisation, mobile health applications
in low-resource settings, digital patient record systems in sub-Saharan Africa, security
in mobile health applications, software development life cycle models, and existing AIbased healthcare systems. The chapter concludes with a synthesis of the identified gaps in
existing knowledge and an explanation of how the present study sought to address those
gaps.
2.2 Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare
Artificial intelligence refers to the simulation of human cognitive functions by computational systems, encompassing capabilities such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving,
and pattern recognition Esteva et al. (2019). Within the healthcare domain, AI has emerged
as a transformative technology, offering tools and methods that support clinical decisionmaking, improve diagnostic accuracy, and enhance the efficiency of healthcare delivery
across a wide range of medical specialties.
The application of AI in healthcare can be broadly categorised into three principal
approaches: machine learning, deep learning, and natural language processing. Machine
learning involves the use of algorithms that learn patterns from structured data without
being explicitly programmed, and has been applied extensively to tasks such as disease
prediction, risk stratification, and patient outcome forecasting. Deep learning, a subset
of machine learning based on artificial neural networks with multiple processing layers,
has demonstrated particularly strong performance in tasks involving unstructured data

9

such as medical images, clinical notes, and audio signals Esteva et al. (2019). Natural
language processing enables machines to understand and generate human language, and
has found application in the analysis of electronic health records, clinical documentation,
and medical literature mining.
Esteva et al. (2019) provided one of the most comprehensive foundational overviews
of deep learning techniques in healthcare, covering applications in computer vision for
medical imaging, natural language processing for electronic health record analysis, reinforcement learning for robotic-assisted surgery, and generalised methods for genomics.
The authors demonstrated that deep learning systems had achieved performance levels
comparable to, and in some cases exceeding, those of expert clinicians in specific diagnostic tasks. However, the authors also acknowledged that the development and deployment of such systems remained predominantly concentrated in high-income countries
with access to large, well-annotated datasets and advanced computational infrastructure.
The applicability of these systems to low-resource settings where data quality is variable
and infrastructure is limited was identified as a critical and underexplored area.
This limitation was further examined by Adepoju et al. (2021), who reviewed the
status and challenges of AI in the African healthcare context. The authors identified
three principal barriers to effective AI deployment in sub-Saharan Africa: the scarcity
of locally representative training data, the absence of robust digital health infrastructure,
and the shortage of AI expertise among local health technology practitioners. The review
concluded that AI systems intended for African healthcare contexts must be designed
with an explicit awareness of local infrastructure constraints, must prioritise operational
reliability under conditions of limited connectivity, and must demonstrate performance
that is robust to the data quality variability inherent in real-world low-resource clinical
settings. These conclusions directly informed the design decisions adopted in the present
study.
The potential of AI to address the diagnostic workforce shortfall in low-resource settings has nonetheless been increasingly recognised Osei et al. (2022) noted that Africa
bears approximately 25% of the global disease burden whilst having access to only 3%
of the world’s healthcare professionals, a disparity that AI-assisted diagnostic tools could
help to mitigate if appropriately adapted for local deployment conditions. The present
study contributed to this effort by developing and evaluating AI models specifically de-

10

signed for deployment under the infrastructure and data quality conditions that characterise healthcare delivery in the North-West Region of Cameroon.
2.3 Deep Learning for Medical Image Analysis
Deep learning refers to a class of machine learning methods that use artificial neural
networks composed of multiple processing layers to learn hierarchical representations of
data directly from raw inputs LeCun et al. (2015). Unlike traditional machine learning approaches, which rely on manually engineered features extracted by domain experts, deep
learning models learn to extract and represent relevant features automatically through exposure to large volumes of labelled training data. This capacity for automatic feature
learning has made deep learning particularly well-suited to the analysis of complex, highdimensional data such as medical images, where the relevant diagnostic features are often
subtle, spatially distributed, and difficult to define explicitly through hand-crafted rules.
LeCun et al. (2015) provided a landmark review of deep learning in their widely cited
paper published in the journal Nature, establishing the theoretical foundations of the field
and demonstrating its transformative potential across a range of domains. The authors
described how deep neural networks learn representations of data with multiple levels
of abstraction, enabling the detection of complex patterns in images, text, and speech
that were previously inaccessible to conventional algorithms. This foundational work
established the basis on which subsequent medical imaging research was built.
The application of deep learning to medical image analysis was comprehensively surveyed by Litjens et al. (2017), who reviewed over 300 contributions to the field covering
tasks including image classification, object detection, segmentation, and image registration across a wide range of clinical application areas. The authors identified convolutional
neural networks as the dominant architectural paradigm for image-based tasks, noting
their ability to exploit the spatial structure of images through the use of learnable convolutional filters applied hierarchically across network layers. The survey demonstrated
that deep learning had achieved state-of-the-art performance in applications spanning radiology, pathology, ophthalmology, and dermatology, and concluded that the field was
undergoing a period of rapid and transformative growth. However, the authors also noted
that the majority of reviewed studies used datasets collected in well-resourced clinical
environments, and that the generalisation of deep learning models to diverse, real-world

11

deployment conditions remained an open and underexplored challenge.
Within medical image analysis, the convolutional neural network represents the most
widely adopted deep learning architecture. A CNN processes an input image through a
series of convolutional layers, each of which applies a set of learnable filters to extract
spatially local features such as edges, textures, and patterns at progressively higher levels
of abstraction. The output of these convolutional layers is typically passed through pooling layers that reduce spatial dimensionality while preserving the most salient feature
activations, and finally through fully connected layers that map the extracted feature representations to output class probabilities Litjens et al. (2017). This hierarchical feature
extraction process allows CNNs to capture both low-level structural details and high-level
semantic content within medical images, making them highly effective for diagnostic
classification tasks.
A significant advancement in CNN architecture came with the introduction of residual networks by He et al. (2016), who proposed a framework that reformulated network
layers as learning residual functions with reference to their inputs. This residual learning
approach enabled the effective training of networks with substantially greater depth than
had previously been feasible, with the authors demonstrating that a 152-layer residual
network achieved a top-5 error rate of 3.57% on the ImageNet classification benchmark,
surpassing all previously reported results. The residual connection mechanism addressed
the vanishing gradient problem that had limited the depth of earlier networks, and the
resulting ResNet architecture has since been widely adopted as a backbone for medical
image analysis tasks, including tuberculosis detection from chest X-rays and malaria detection from blood smear microscopy images He et al. (2016).
However, the computational demands of deep residual networks and similar largescale architectures present a significant challenge for deployment on mobile devices with
limited processing power and memory Howard et al. (2017) addressed this challenge
through the introduction of MobileNets, a family of lightweight CNN architectures specifically designed for efficient inference on mobile and embedded systems. MobileNets
employ depthwise separable convolutions, which factorise standard convolutions into a
depthwise convolution applied independently to each input channel followed by a pointwise convolution that combines the channel outputs. This factorisation substantially reduces the number of parameters and computational operations required, whilst preserving

12

a large proportion of the representational capacity of the equivalent standard convolution.
Howard et al. Howard et al. (2017) demonstrated that MobileNets achieved competitive
accuracy on standard image classification benchmarks whilst requiring significantly fewer
floating-point operations than comparable architectures, making them a practical choice
for on-device inference in resource-constrained environments.
The relevance of these architectural developments to the present study is direct. The
computational efficiency of MobileNetV2, an improved version of the original MobileNet
architecture, made it a suitable backbone for the CNN models developed in this study,
where deployment on mobile devices under conditions of limited computational resources
was a primary design constraint. The use of residual connections within the MobileNetV2
architecture further contributed to training stability and model performance, particularly
given the relatively small size of the tuberculosis and malaria training datasets employed
in this study.
A key consideration in the application of deep learning to medical image analysis is
the requirement for large, well-annotated training datasets to achieve robust model performance. In practice, the collection and annotation of medical imaging data is costly,
time-consuming, and subject to significant regulatory and ethical constraints. Transfer
learning has emerged as the principal strategy for addressing this data scarcity challenge,
whereby a model pre-trained on a large general-purpose dataset such as ImageNet is subsequently fine-tuned on a smaller domain-specific medical imaging dataset Litjens et al.
(2017). The pre-trained model retains the low-level and mid-level feature representations learned from the large general dataset, which are then adapted through fine-tuning
to the specific characteristics of the target medical imaging task. This approach has been
demonstrated to yield substantial improvements in model performance under limited data
conditions compared to training from scratch, and was adopted in the present study for
both the tuberculosis and malaria detection models.
Despite these advances, the application of deep learning to medical image analysis in
low-resource settings introduces challenges that are not adequately addressed by the existing literature. The majority of published deep learning models for medical image analysis
were trained and evaluated using datasets collected under controlled, high-quality imaging
conditions. When such models are deployed in low-resource environments where imaging
equipment is rudimentary and image acquisition conditions are variable, a phenomenon

13

known as domain shift occurs, in which the statistical distribution of the deployment data
differs significantly from that of the training data, resulting in a degradation of model
performance Sendra-Balcells et al. (2023). The present study directly addressed this challenge by incorporating domain-shift-aware data augmentation strategies into the model
training pipeline, as described in detail in Chapter 3.
2.4 Convolutional Neural Networks for Tuberculosis Detection
Tuberculosis is a chronic infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium
tuberculosis, primarily affecting the lungs and manifesting radiologically as abnormalities in chest X-ray images including infiltrates, cavitation, nodules, and pleural effusion
World Health Organisation (2023a). The interpretation of these radiological features traditionally requires the expertise of a trained radiologist, a resource that is consistently
unavailable at the peripheral healthcare level in low-income countries such as Cameroon.
The application of convolutional neural networks to the automated detection of tuberculosis from chest X-ray images has therefore attracted significant research attention as a
means of extending diagnostic capability to resource-constrained settings.
The foundational datasets underpinning much of the research in this area were introduced by Jaeger et al. (2014), who published two publicly available chest X-ray datasets
on behalf of the United States National Library of Medicine. The first dataset, collected
from the Department of Health and Human Services of Montgomery County, Maryland,
contains 138 posterior-anterior chest X-ray images, of which 58 are positive for tuberculosis and 80 are normal. The second dataset, collected from Shenzhen No. 3 People’s
Hospital in collaboration with Guangdong Medical College in China, contains 662 chest
X-ray images, of which 336 are positive for tuberculosis and 326 are normal. Both datasets contain associated radiologist readings and were made publicly available to foster research in computer-aided diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis. These two datasets were
selected for use in the present study due to their public availability, established use in
the literature, and the diversity of tuberculosis presentations they collectively represent
Jaeger et al. (2014) noted that the datasets were collected under real-world clinical conditions, introducing natural variability in image quality, patient positioning, and radiological
presentation that makes them a realistic basis for model training and evaluation.
Subsequent research demonstrated the feasibility of deep learning for tuberculosis

14

detection using these and related datasets Rajpurkar et al. (2017) proposed CheXNet, a
121-layer densely connected CNN trained on the NIH Chest X-ray dataset containing
over 100,000 chest X-ray images labelled across 14 pathological conditions, including
pneumonia, infiltration, and effusion. The model achieved an F1 score of 0.435 on the
pneumonia detection task, which the authors reported exceeded the mean performance of
a panel of four board-certified radiologists at 0.387. Whilst CheXNet was not specifically
developed for tuberculosis detection, the study was landmark in demonstrating that deep
learning models trained at scale could achieve clinician-level performance on chest Xray interpretation, establishing the theoretical basis for subsequent tuberculosis-specific
models. However, the study relied on an exceptionally large dataset collected from a
major United States hospital, a condition that is not reproducible in low-resource settings
such as Cameroon.
Rajaraman and Antani (2020) addressed the challenge of tuberculosis detection more
directly by proposing an ensemble of modality-specific deep learning models evaluated
on the Shenzhen and Montgomery datasets. The authors fine-tuned multiple pre-trained
CNN architectures including VGG-16, InceptionV3, and Xception, and combined their
predictions through an ensemble strategy that leveraged the complementary strengths
of each model. The ensemble approach achieved an area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve of 0.99 on the Shenzhen dataset and 0.98 on the Montgomery dataset, demonstrating that transfer learning from large general-purpose image datasets combined with domain-specific fine-tuning could achieve near-perfect discrimination between
tuberculosis-positive and normal chest X-rays on these benchmarks. The authors further
noted that the modality-specific training approach, in which models were fine-tuned specifically on chest X-ray data rather than general natural images, yielded substantially improved performance compared to direct application of ImageNet-pretrained models. This
finding informed the transfer learning strategy adopted in the present study.
Rahman et al. (2020) conducted a comprehensive study employing nine different deep
CNN architectures for tuberculosis detection, including ResNet18, ResNet50, ResNet101,
CheXNet, InceptionV3, VGG19, DenseNet201, SqueezeNet, and MobileNet, trained on a
combined dataset of 700 tuberculosis-positive and 3,500 normal chest X-ray images. The
study also incorporated lung segmentation as a preprocessing step, isolating the lung regions prior to classification. The best-performing model achieved an accuracy of 99.9%,

15

sensitivity of 99.9%, and specificity of 99.52% when applied to segmented lung images,
compared to an accuracy of 97.07% and sensitivity of 97.07% on unsegmented whole
X-ray images. The authors concluded that segmentation of the lung region prior to classification substantially improved detection performance by reducing the influence of nondiagnostic image regions such as the ribcage and soft tissue. Whilst these results are
impressive, the authors acknowledged that the dataset used was compiled from multiple
public sources and therefore did not reflect the image quality variability characteristic of
real-world clinical settings in low-resource environments. Furthermore, the study evaluated MobileNet as one of the architectures, reporting competitive performance relative to
much larger models, which supported the selection of the MobileNetV2 architecture in
the present study on grounds of both accuracy and computational efficiency.
Santosh et al. (2022) conducted a systematic review of deep learning techniques for
tuberculosis screening from chest X-rays covering publications from the preceding five
years. The review identified several consistent findings across the surveyed literature.
First, transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained models consistently outperformed training from scratch, particularly when the available tuberculosis-specific training dataset was
small, as is the case with the Shenzhen and Montgomery datasets. Second, data augmentation strategies including random rotation, horizontal flipping, brightness adjustment, and
zoom were widely employed to mitigate overfitting and improve model generalisation under limited data conditions. Third, and most relevantly for the present study, the authors
identified domain shift as a critical and largely unaddressed challenge, noting that the
majority of published models had been evaluated exclusively on controlled benchmark
datasets and that their performance under real-world clinical imaging conditions, particularly in low-resource settings, remained largely unknown. The review explicitly called
for research addressing the robustness of tuberculosis detection models to the image quality variability and acquisition condition variability that characterise healthcare settings in
low-income countries, directly identifying the research gap that the present study sought
to address.
Despite the strong performance reported in the literature, several limitations constrain
the applicability of existing tuberculosis detection models to the deployment context of
the present study. First, the majority of high-performing models employ large, computationally intensive architectures such as DenseNet201, ResNet101, and InceptionV3

16

that are not feasible for on-device inference on mid-range mobile phones with limited
processing power and memory. Second, most studies report performance on standardised benchmark datasets and do not evaluate model robustness under conditions of image
degradation or variable acquisition quality, which are characteristic of chest X-rays captured using portable or low-cost equipment in peripheral health facilities. Third, none of
the reviewed studies address the specific challenge of deploying tuberculosis detection
models in a hybrid offline and online mobile application architecture suitable for environments with unreliable internet connectivity. The present study addressed all three of these
limitations by selecting the computationally efficient MobileNetV2 architecture, incorporating domain-shift-aware augmentation strategies to improve robustness to degraded
image quality, and integrating the trained model within a hybrid offline and online mobile
application designed for deployment in the North-West Region of Cameroon.
2.5 Convolutional Neural Networks for Malaria Detection
Malaria is a life-threatening infectious disease caused by Plasmodium parasites transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. The disease
disproportionately affects populations in sub-Saharan Africa, which accounted for 93.6%
of all global malaria cases and 95.4% of all malaria-related deaths in 2022, with an estimated 249 million cases and 608,000 fatalities reported globally in that year World Health
Organisation (2023b). The microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained thin blood smear
slides remains the current gold standard for malaria diagnosis, enabling the identification and classification of Plasmodium parasites within red blood cells at different stages
of their life cycle, including the ring, trophozoite, schizont, and gametocyte stages Rajaraman et al. (2018). However, this diagnostic process is inherently dependent on the
availability of trained microscopists, well-maintained microscopes, and consistent slide
preparation quality, conditions that are rarely met simultaneously in peripheral health facilities across sub-Saharan Africa and Cameroon specifically. The application of convolutional neural networks to automate malaria parasite detection from blood smear images
has therefore attracted substantial and growing research interest as a means of reducing
this dependence on scarce skilled personnel.
The foundational dataset for CNN-based malaria detection research was developed
by researchers at the Lister Hill National Center for Biomedical Communications at the

17

United States National Library of Medicine and published alongside the landmark study
by Rajaraman et al. (2018). The dataset, herein referred to as the NIH Malaria Cell Images dataset, was created from Giemsa-stained thin blood smear slides collected from
150 patients infected with Plasmodium falciparum and 50 healthy patients. Images of
slides were captured using a smartphone camera for each microscopic field of view, and
individual cell images were extracted using a level-set based segmentation algorithm. Expert slide readers at the Mahidol-Oxford Tropical Medicine Research Unit in Bangkok,
Thailand manually annotated all images. The resulting dataset contains a total of 27,558
segmented cell images, comprising 13,779 parasitised images and 13,779 uninfected images, distributed in a perfectly balanced binary classification format. The dataset was
made publicly available through the National Library of Medicine and has since become
the most widely used benchmark for malaria detection research. This dataset was selected
for use in the present study due to its public availability, its balanced class distribution,
its established use as a benchmark across the literature, and the critical observation that
the original images were captured using a smartphone camera held to a microscope eyepiece, which closely mirrors the image acquisition conditions anticipated in the target
deployment environment of Cameroonian health facilities.
Rajaraman et al. (2018) evaluated the performance of multiple pre-trained CNN architectures as feature extractors for malaria parasite classification using the NIH dataset. The
architectures evaluated included AlexNet, VGG-16, Xception, ResNet-50, and a custom
CNN, each fine-tuned on the malaria cell image dataset. The study found that all pretrained models substantially outperformed the custom CNN trained from scratch, demonstrating the clear benefit of transfer learning for this task. The best-performing model, a
fine-tuned Xception network, achieved an accuracy of 95.9%, a sensitivity of 95.6%, a
specificity of 96.2%, and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.99
on the test set. The authors concluded that pre-trained CNNs used as feature extractors,
combined with fine-tuning on domain-specific data, provided a highly effective and practically viable approach to automated malaria detection. This finding directly informed the
transfer learning strategy adopted in the present study.
Subsequent studies further advanced the performance of CNN-based malaria detection models on the NIH dataset Siłka et al. (2023) proposed a novel CNN architecture
that achieved a classification accuracy of 99.68% on the NIH Malaria Cell Images data-

18

set, with high sensitivity and specificity. The authors attributed this performance to the use
of residual connections, dropout regularisation, and careful hyperparameter optimisation.
Similarly, a systematic review by Yusuf et al. (2024) covering peer-reviewed publications

for digital malaria diagnosis, achieving peak accuracies of 99.12%, and noted that custom
CNN-based models were used in 58% of reviewed studies. The review further identified the NIH dataset as the most widely used benchmark, appearing in the majority of
reviewed studies, and concluded that deep learning had achieved a level of performance
sufficient for clinical support applications. However, the review also noted that most highperforming models had been developed and evaluated under controlled dataset conditions
and had not been tested for robustness to real-world image quality variability.
A particularly important strand of research for the present study concerns the application of deep learning to malaria detection specifically under the image acquisition conditions characteristic of low-resource clinical settings. The original NIH dataset, whilst

More recently, research has begun to address the phone-captured microscopy challenge directly. A study by Kanafani et al. (2024) employed the ConvNeXt architecture
with transfer learning and augmentation strategies specifically designed to simulate the

that models trained with these degradation-aware augmentation strategies substantially

Despite the strong body of research demonstrating the feasibility of CNN-based malaria detection, several critical gaps remain. First, whilst the NIH Malaria Cell Images
dataset has been extensively used, the majority of studies employ it without accounting for

of a malaria detection model within a hybrid offline and online mobile health application

of the computationally efficient MobileNetV2 architecture, the adoption of domain-shiftaware augmentation strategies informed by the literature on phone-captured microscopy,
and the integration of the trained model within a hybrid mobile application designed for
deployment in the North-West Region of Cameroon.
2.6 Handling Degraded and Noisy Medical Images
The performance of deep learning models for medical image classification is critically
dependent on the quality and representativeness of the data on which they are trained. In
clinical practice, and particularly in low-resource settings, medical images are frequently
subject to a range of degradation factors that reduce their quality relative to the controlled
datasets typically used for model training. These factors include poor illumination, motion blur, variable focus, artefacts introduced by low-cost imaging equipment, inconsistent
staining in histological preparations, and, in the context of the present study, the optical
distortions and vignetting introduced when images are captured using a smartphone camera held manually to a microscope eyepiece. The capacity of a deep learning model to
maintain reliable diagnostic performance in the presence of such degradation is referred
to as robustness, and it constitutes one of the most significant and practically import-

20

ant challenges in the deployment of medical AI systems in low-resource environments
Sendra-Balcells et al. (2023).
The domain shift problem, wherein the statistical distribution of images encountered
at deployment differs from that of the training dataset, is the primary mechanism through
which image degradation degrades model performance Guan and Liu (2021) provided a
comprehensive survey of domain adaptation methods in medical image analysis, categorising sources of domain shift into demographic shifts arising from patient heterogeneity,
and protocol and modality shifts arising from differences in imaging equipment, acquisition parameters, noise characteristics, and image resolution. The authors reviewed a broad
range of domain adaptation strategies and concluded that, whilst significant progress had
been made in developing methods to address domain shift in well-resourced research
settings, the application of these methods to genuinely low-resource deployment environments remained an open and insufficiently studied problem. This conclusion is directly
applicable to the deployment context of the present study, where both the tuberculosis
detection model and the malaria detection model are trained on publicly available datasets collected under conditions that differ substantively from those of peripheral health
facilities in Cameroon.
Data augmentation is the most widely adopted and practically accessible strategy for
improving model robustness to image degradation and domain shift Chlap et al. (2021)
conducted a systematic review of data augmentation techniques for medical imaging deep
learning applications, covering geometric transformations, intensity transformations, and
generative model-based augmentation approaches. The authors identified geometric transformations including random rotation, flipping, cropping, and affine distortion as the most
commonly applied augmentation strategies, and noted that intensity transformations including brightness adjustment, contrast variation, Gaussian noise addition, and colour jitter were particularly effective for improving robustness to the kind of imaging variability
encountered across different clinical environments and equipment types. The review concluded that data augmentation consistently improved model generalisation when applied
appropriately, but emphasised that augmentation strategies must be designed with an explicit understanding of the specific degradation patterns expected in the target deployment
environment rather than applied generically. This finding directly guided the design of the
augmentation pipeline adopted in the present study, where each augmentation transform

21

was selected to simulate a specific and realistic source of image degradation expected in
the Cameroonian clinical context.
For the malaria detection model specifically, the primary sources of image degradation anticipated in the deployment environment arise from the use of a smartphone camera
held to the eyepiece of a basic light microscope. These include circular vignetting from
the microscope eyepiece aperture, uneven illumination across the field of view, colour
distortion introduced by the smartphone’s automatic white balance algorithm, variable
focus resulting from the lack of a fixed camera-to-eyepiece mount, and motion blur from
hand tremor during image capture Kanafani et al. (2024) demonstrated that CNN models for malaria detection trained without these degradation-aware augmentations exhibited substantial performance degradation when evaluated on images captured via smartphone through a microscope eyepiece, and that models trained with targeted augmentation
strategies designed to simulate phone-captured microscopy conditions substantially outperformed the baseline. This study provides direct methodological justification for the
degradation-aware augmentation approach adopted for the malaria detection model in the
present study.
For the tuberculosis detection model, the principal sources of image degradation in
the target deployment environment arise from the use of low-cost or portable digital Xray equipment, variable patient positioning, and suboptimal exposure settings Rahman
et al. (2020) demonstrated that data augmentation including random rotation, horizontal
flipping, brightness adjustment, zoom, and shear transformation substantially improved
tuberculosis detection model performance on the Shenzhen and Montgomery datasets.
The authors noted that augmentation was particularly important given the relatively small
size of these datasets, where the risk of overfitting without augmentation was considerable
Santosh et al. (2022) similarly concluded from their five-year systematic review that data
augmentation was a consistent and essential component of high-performing tuberculosis
detection models, and that the specific choice of augmentation parameters should reflect
the expected variability in image quality across clinical deployment environments.
Beyond geometric and intensity-based augmentation, MixUp training, introduced by
Zhang et al. (2018), represents an additional strategy for improving model robustness
to ambiguous and degraded inputs. MixUp generates virtual training examples by computing convex combinations of pairs of training images and their corresponding labels,

22

thereby training the model to produce smooth and well-calibrated output probability distributions between class boundaries rather than learning sharp, memorised decision boundaries. The technique was grounded in the Vicinal Risk Minimisation principle, which proposes that models trained on examples in the vicinity of training data points rather than the
training points themselves exhibit improved generalisation and robustness to distribution
shift Zhang et al. (2018) demonstrated that MixUp training substantially improved model
accuracy and robustness to label noise on standard image classification benchmarks. In
the context of medical image classification, where degraded images may present ambiguous diagnostic features that do not clearly belong to either the positive or negative class,
MixUp training provides a principled mechanism for improving model calibration and
robustness. This technique was adopted in the present study for both the tuberculosis and
malaria detection models, complementing the geometric and intensity-based augmentation pipeline.
Despite the availability of these techniques, a significant gap remains in the literature. The majority of studies evaluating augmentation and domain adaptation strategies
for medical image classification do so in well-resourced research environments using
high-quality baseline images as the starting point for degradation simulation. Very few
studies have evaluated the combined effect of multiple degradation-aware augmentation
strategies, MixUp training, and transfer learning specifically within the context of mobile
AI deployment for infectious disease diagnosis in sub-Saharan Africa. The present study
addressed this gap by designing and evaluating an integrated robustness pipeline combining all three strategies for both the tuberculosis and malaria detection models, and by
comparing model performance with and without this pipeline to quantify its contribution
to diagnostic accuracy under simulated real-world deployment conditions.
2.7 Mobile Artificial Intelligence and Model Optimisation
The deployment of deep learning models on mobile devices presents a set of technical challenges that are fundamentally different from those encountered in server-based
or cloud-based AI deployment. Mobile devices are characterised by limited computational processing power, constrained memory capacity, restricted battery life, and variable or absent internet connectivity. These constraints make it impractical to deploy the
large, computationally intensive deep learning architectures that achieve state-of-the-art

23

performance on standard benchmarks directly on a smartphone without significant optimisation. The field of mobile AI, also referred to as on-device or edge AI, has therefore
developed a range of model compression and optimisation techniques specifically designed to reduce the computational and memory requirements of deep learning models
whilst preserving as much of their predictive accuracy as possible Xu et al. (2023).
The MobileNetV2 architecture, introduced by Sandler et al. (2018) at the IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition, represents one of the most significant contributions to mobile AI and was the primary architectural choice for both the
tuberculosis and malaria detection models developed in the present study. MobileNetV2
builds upon the original MobileNet architecture by introducing two key innovations: inverted residual blocks and linear bottlenecks. In the inverted residual structure, the input
and output of each residual block are thin, low-dimensional representations, whilst the
intermediate expansion layer uses lightweight depthwise separable convolutions to filter
features in a higher-dimensional space. This design is the inverse of the traditional residual block used in ResNet architectures, where the input and output are high-dimensional
and the bottleneck is compressed. The linear bottleneck removes the non-linear activation
function from the output of the bottleneck layer, preserving the representational power of
the low-dimensional feature manifold Sandler et al. (2018) demonstrated that MobileNetV2 achieved state-of-the-art performance on the ImageNet classification benchmark and
on object detection and semantic segmentation tasks whilst requiring substantially fewer
multiply-add operations and parameters than comparable architectures, making it highly
suitable for on-device inference on mobile hardware. The architecture has since become
one of the most widely adopted backbones for mobile medical AI applications due to this
combination of accuracy and computational efficiency.
Model quantisation is the principal technique applied to further reduce the computational and memory footprint of a trained deep learning model for mobile deployment.
Quantisation involves converting the floating-point numerical representations used during model training, typically 32-bit floating-point values, to lower-precision integer representations, most commonly 8-bit integers, for use during inference. This conversion
reduces the model size by a factor of approximately four and accelerates inference by
exploiting the efficiency of integer arithmetic operations on mobile processors, which
are optimised for integer rather than floating-point computation Deng et al. (2020). Wu

24

et al. (2020) conducted a comprehensive empirical evaluation of integer quantisation for
deep learning inference and demonstrated that post-training dynamic quantisation of convolutional neural networks could achieve near-baseline accuracy whilst reducing model
size and improving inference latency by approximately three to four times on standard
mobile benchmarks. The authors noted that MobileNet-family architectures were particularly well-suited to post-training quantisation due to their use of depthwise separable
convolutions, which quantise with minimal accuracy degradation. Post-training dynamic
quantisation was adopted in the present study for both the tuberculosis and malaria detection models, converting trained model weights from float32 to int8 precision prior to
mobile deployment.
TorchScript is the serialisation and compilation framework provided by the PyTorch
deep learning library for exporting trained models to a platform-independent, self-contained
format that can be executed independently of the Python runtime environment. The TorchScript format enables a trained PyTorch model to be loaded and executed directly within
a mobile application written in Java or Kotlin for Android, or Swift for iOS, without
requiring any Python dependencies on the device. The PyTorch Mobile framework,
which provides the on-device inference runtime for TorchScript models, supports both
Android and iOS platforms and includes the mobile optimiser utility, which applies a set
of graph-level optimisations to the TorchScript model specifically targeting mobile inference performance, including operator fusion, memory layout optimisation, and removal
of operations unnecessary for inference PyTorch Foundation (2023). The combination
of post-training quantisation and TorchScript export with mobile optimisation constituted
the model deployment pipeline adopted in the present study, enabling both the tuberculosis and malaria detection models to be embedded directly within the Flutter mobile
application for on-device offline inference.
The clinical relevance of on-device inference for mobile health applications in lowresource settings has been demonstrated by several studies. Kassim et al. Kassim et al.
(2021) developed a deep learning platform for automated malaria screening from thin
blood smear images that was specifically designed to operate exclusively on smartphone
hardware without requiring internet connectivity, taking advantage of the widespread
availability of low-end smartphones across the African continent. The authors demonstrated that a lightweight CNN model deployed on-device could achieve clinically ac-

25

ceptable accuracy for malaria screening whilst eliminating the dependence on cloud infrastructure that limits the deployability of many AI health tools in sub-Saharan Africa.
This study provided direct evidence that on-device mobile AI deployment for infectious
disease diagnosis in African healthcare settings was both technically feasible and clinically meaningful, and reinforced the architectural decisions made in the present study.
Kirefu et al. (2021) conducted a scoping review of mobile health technologies for disease diagnosis and treatment support by health workers in sub-Saharan Africa, reviewing
evidence from the inception of mobile health technology to April 2020. The review identified that the majority of mobile health applications used in the region relied on internet
connectivity for data transmission and processing, and that the absence of offline functionality was consistently identified as a barrier to adoption in rural and low-connectivity
environments. The authors concluded that the development of mobile health tools with
robust offline functionality was a critical priority for improving healthcare delivery in
sub-Saharan Africa. This conclusion directly motivated the hybrid offline and online architecture adopted in the present study, which ensures that AI-assisted diagnosis remains
available to clinicians regardless of network availability.
Despite these advances, significant gaps remain in the literature on mobile AI for
healthcare in low-resource African settings. Existing studies have primarily evaluated
single-disease mobile diagnostic tools and have not addressed the integration of multiple
AI models, digital patient records, and clinical chat functionality within a single secure
mobile application. Furthermore, no existing study has evaluated the performance differential between on-device quantised models and full-precision server-based models in the
context of mobile health deployment, nor has any study developed a hybrid offline and online inference architecture that automatically selects between on-device and server-based
inference based on network availability whilst flagging the source of each result to the
clinician. These gaps constitute the technical contributions of the present study.
2.8 Mobile Health Applications in Low-Resource Settings
2.8.1 Overview of Mobile Health
Mobile health, commonly abbreviated as mHealth, is defined by the World Health
Organisation as medical and public health practice supported by mobile devices, such as
mobile phones, patient monitoring devices, personal digital assistants, and other wireless

26

devices World Health Organisation (2011). More broadly, mHealth encompasses the use
of mobile and wireless technologies to support health objectives, including the collection
of community and clinical health data, the delivery of healthcare information to practitioners, researchers, and patients, the remote monitoring of patient vital signs, and the
direct provision of care through mobile telemedicine platforms McCool et al. (2022). The
emergence of mHealth as a distinct field of healthcare delivery has been driven by three
converging forces: the rapid global proliferation of mobile devices, the continuous advancement of mobile application development frameworks, and the growing recognition
that mobile technologies offer a uniquely scalable and cost-effective means of extending
healthcare access to populations that are geographically or economically underserved by
conventional health infrastructure.
The global mHealth market was estimated at approximately 17.92 billion United
States dollars in 2019 and was projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate of
45% through to 2027, reflecting the scale of investment and interest in mobile health technologies across both high-income and low-income country contexts Grand View Research
(2020). Applications of mHealth span a wide range of clinical and public health functions,
including disease surveillance, treatment adherence support, remote patient monitoring,
health worker training and communication, and AI-assisted diagnostic support. For the
purposes of the present study, the most relevant application domain is AI-assisted clinical diagnosis and patient record management delivered through a mobile platform, with
specific attention to the design requirements for deployment in low-resource settings characterised by unreliable internet connectivity.
2.8.2 mHealth Adoption in Sub-Saharan Africa
Sub-Saharan Africa represents both the region of greatest need for mHealth solutions
and the region of most significant growth in mobile technology adoption. The combination of a disproportionately high disease burden, a severe shortage of healthcare workers,
and rapidly increasing smartphone penetration creates a compelling context for mHealth
deployment. Betjeman et al. Betjeman et al. (2013) demonstrated in an early review
that mHealth applications in sub-Saharan Africa had shown consistent benefits in patient
monitoring, medication adherence, and healthcare worker communication, particularly in
rural areas where fixed health infrastructure was limited. These findings established a
foundational evidence base for the potential of mHealth to bridge the healthcare access
27

gap in the region.
More recent evidence confirms and extends these findings. Kirefu et al. Kirefu et al.
(2021) conducted a scoping review of mobile health technologies for disease diagnosis
and treatment support by health workers in sub-Saharan Africa and found that mHealth
tools had improved the timeliness and accuracy of disease reporting, facilitated data collection and patient tracking, and supported clinical decision-making at the community
health worker level. The review identified malaria, tuberculosis, HIV, and maternal health
as the most common disease areas addressed by mHealth interventions in the region.
Eze et al. Eze et al. (2023) conducted a further scoping review of current evidence on
mHealth approaches in sub-Saharan Africa and found that whilst less than half of the 48
sub-Saharan African countries were making substantive attempts to incorporate mHealth
platforms into their formal health systems, rapid progress in both mobile infrastructure
and mHealth-related research suggested that the conditions for wider scale-up of promising mHealth programmes were increasingly being met. The authors identified patients,
caregivers, and healthcare workers as the typical end-user groups across reviewed studies,
and noted that user-centred design was a consistent predictor of adoption success.
Cameroon specifically has seen limited but growing engagement with mHealth solutions. The teleconsultation platform Waspito, discussed in detail in Section 2.12.3, represents the most prominent locally developed mHealth initiative, connecting patients with
healthcare providers through digital channels. However, formal integration of mHealth
tools into the national health system remains nascent, and the majority of peripheral health
facilities in Cameroon continue to rely exclusively on paper-based record systems and
manual diagnostic processes without digital support tools.
2.8.3 Challenges of mHealth Deployment in Low-Connectivity Environments
Despite the evident potential of mHealth in sub-Saharan Africa, the practical deployment of mobile health applications in low-resource settings encounters a consistent set of
barriers that have limited adoption and sustained use. Aboye et al. Aboye et al. (2024)
conducted a qualitative study assessing barriers and enablers of mHealth system adoption
in sub-Saharan Africa from the perspectives of patients, physicians, and healthcare executives, and identified infrastructural barriers as the most significant category of impediment. These included poor or inefficient network systems, unreliable electricity supply,
and insufficient device maintenance support. The authors noted that physicians specific28

ally identified the absence of locally contextualised digital health systems and concerns
about patients’ capacity to engage with mHealth tools as barriers to adoption, whilst patients identified the need for automated health monitoring tools as a key enabler.
Omenka et al. Omenka et al. (2023) conducted a scoping review of healthcare workers’ perspectives on mHealth adoption in the developing world and similarly found that
infrastructure-related barriers, particularly poor network coverage and unreliable power
supply, were the most consistently reported impediments to sustained mHealth adoption.
The review noted that financial costs associated with device ownership, data subscription, and application maintenance further constrained adoption, particularly among community health workers who frequently bore these costs personally. These findings are
directly applicable to the deployment context of the present study in the North-West Region of Cameroon, where network coverage is intermittent, particularly in rural areas and
in communities affected by the ongoing Anglophone crisis.
McCool et al. McCool et al. (2022) reviewed the trajectory of mHealth in low- and
middle-income countries and cautioned that the frequently cited optimism about the transformative potential of mobile technologies for health must be tempered by the persistent
reality of infrastructural constraints, digital literacy gaps, and the tendency for mHealth
programmes to remain in proof-of-concept or pilot stages without achieving sustained
scale. The authors argued that future mHealth development in low-income country contexts must prioritise offline functionality, low-bandwidth operation, and contextually appropriate user interface design to achieve genuine impact.
2.8.4 Offline-Capable Mobile Health Applications
The design of offline-capable mobile health applications, sometimes referred to as
offline-first applications, has emerged as a critical response to the connectivity challenges
documented in the literature. An offline-first application is one that is designed to function fully without internet connectivity as its primary mode of operation, synchronising
data with a central server only when connectivity becomes available. This design philosophy inverts the default assumption of internet connectivity that underlies most commercially developed mobile applications, replacing it with an assumption of intermittent
or absent connectivity that better reflects the operational reality of healthcare settings in
low-resource environments.
Thambawita et al. Thambawita et al. (2022) demonstrated the practical value of the
29

offline-first design approach in a digital health record application deployed in a lowresource primary care setting, reporting that offline-first design substantially improved
application reliability and user adoption by ensuring that clinical data could always be
recorded regardless of network status, with automatic synchronisation occurring when
connectivity was restored. The authors reported a median patient registration time of 76
seconds and follow-up visit entry of 13 seconds, demonstrating that offline-first design
was compatible with the time-constrained workflow of busy health facilities.
The integration of on-device AI inference with offline-first data management represents the natural extension of this design approach to AI-assisted diagnostics. Kassim et al.
Kassim et al. (2021) demonstrated that a deep learning model for malaria detection could
be deployed directly on a smartphone to operate without any internet dependency, making AI-assisted diagnosis available in environments where cloud-based inference would
be impractical. This study confirmed that the combination of model quantisation, lightweight architecture selection, and on-device inference was sufficient to deliver clinically
acceptable diagnostic performance on standard Android smartphones without requiring
specialised hardware.
The hybrid offline and online architecture adopted in the present study builds directly
on these foundations. By embedding quantised MobileNetV2 models within the Flutter mobile application for on-device inference and synchronising results with the Django
backend server when connectivity is available, the system ensures that AI-assisted diagnosis of tuberculosis and malaria remains continuously available to clinicians regardless
of network status. The hybrid approach further addresses the performance differential
between on-device compressed models and full-precision server-based models by flagging the source of each diagnostic result to the clinician, ensuring transparency about the
confidence level associated with each inference context.
2.9 Digital Patient Record Systems in Sub-Saharan Africa
2.9.1 Limitations of Paper-Based Patient Records
The paper-based patient record, manifested in Cameroon and many other sub-Saharan
African countries as a physical consultation booklet carried by the patient between health
facilities, remains the predominant mechanism for documenting clinical encounters at the
peripheral healthcare level. Whilst familiar and requiring no technological infrastructure,

30

paper-based record systems impose a consistent and well-documented set of limitations
on the quality and continuity of healthcare delivery in low-resource settings.
Mutia et al. (2025) identified the accuracy and comprehensiveness of medical data as
the primary problems with paper-based systems in developing country healthcare contexts, noting that handwritten records are frequently incomplete, illegible, or inconsistently structured, making them unreliable as a basis for clinical decision-making. The
consequences of this unreliability are clinically significant. When a patient presents at
a health facility without their consultation booklet, or when the booklet has been lost or
damaged, the attending clinician has no access to the patient’s medical history, previous
diagnoses, or prior treatment records. This situation forces clinical decisions to be made
on the basis of incomplete information, increasing the risk of diagnostic error, inappropriate treatment, and adverse drug interactions.
The problem of data fragmentation is particularly acute in settings like the North-West
Region of Cameroon, where patients frequently move between health facilities depending
on proximity, availability of services, and the disruptions caused by the ongoing Anglophone crisis. Ibeneme et al. Ibeneme et al. (2023) noted that patient data in much of
sub-Saharan Africa remains fragmented across facilities, programmes, and paper records,
limiting the ability of clinicians to make informed decisions at the point of care and resulting in a system where clinical history is often incomplete, treatment pathways are difficult to track, and avoidable inefficiencies are built into everyday service delivery. The
duplication of diagnostic tests resulting from unavailable prior records further imposes a
measurable financial burden on health systems that can ill afford unnecessary expenditure.
Beyond the clinical consequences, paper-based records impose operational burdens on
health facilities in terms of physical storage requirements, retrieval time, and the risk of
record loss or destruction through fire, flooding, or conflict-related damage, all of which
are realistic threats in the healthcare context of the North-West Region of Cameroon.
The transition to digital patient record systems has therefore been identified as a fundamental priority for improving healthcare delivery across sub-Saharan Africa Adeleke
et al. (2025).
2.9.2 Electronic Health Record Systems in Low-Resource Settings
Electronic health record systems, defined as web-based or software-based computerised medical information systems that collect, store, and display patient information in a
31

paperless format accessible only to authorised personnel, have been widely recognised as
the primary technological solution to the limitations of paper-based records Mohammed
et al. (2025). The benefits of electronic health record adoption include improved data
accuracy and completeness, enhanced continuity of care through longitudinal patient records accessible across facilities, support for clinical decision-making through structured
data presentation, and the generation of aggregated health data for population-level surveillance and programme management Adeleke et al. (2025).
Despite these acknowledged benefits, the penetration of electronic health record systems in sub-Saharan Africa remains low and highly uneven. Adeleke et al. Adeleke et al.
(2025) conducted a comprehensive scoping review of a decade of electronic health record
implementation in sub-Saharan Africa and found that whilst EHR systems had demonstrated significant benefits in improved data management, reduced errors, and enhanced
clinical decision-making in the studies where they were successfully implemented, the
majority of implementations had encountered persistent challenges related to limited infrastructure, financial constraints, data privacy concerns, and insufficient training of health
personnel. The review identified unreliable electricity supply and inconsistent internet
connectivity as the most commonly cited infrastructure barriers, followed by the absence
of locally contextualised system design and the resistance of healthcare workers to adopting new technologies that disrupted established workflows.
The dominance of HIV treatment programmes as the primary driver of electronic
health record adoption in sub-Saharan Africa is a significant limitation of the existing
evidence base Mwangi et al. (2014) noted that approximately 91% of reported electronic
health record implementations in the region at that time were associated with HIV-related
health centres, reflecting the influence of international funding programmes rather than
systemic national digital health strategies. Whilst more recent implementations have begun to extend beyond HIV to other disease areas, the integration of electronic health
records into the general primary and district hospital care workflow, which is the level
targeted by the present study, remains an area of significant unmet need across the region,
including in Cameroon.
Large-scale electronic health record systems such as OpenMRS, which has been widely
deployed across sub-Saharan Africa, offer comprehensive functionality but require significant technical infrastructure, dedicated server hardware, reliable connectivity, and

32

trained system administrators to deploy and maintain. These requirements place full-scale
electronic health record implementation beyond the practical reach of most peripheral
health facilities in low-resource environments, including district hospitals and health centres
in Cameroon. This implementation gap between the demonstrated benefits of electronic
health records and the practical feasibility of deploying existing systems at the peripheral
healthcare level constitutes a significant unmet need that the digital patient record component of the present study sought to address.
2.9.3 Mobile-Based Patient Record Solutions
The recognition that full-scale electronic health record systems are impractical for
many peripheral health facilities in sub-Saharan Africa has driven growing interest in
lightweight, mobile-based patient record solutions that operate on smartphones without
requiring dedicated server infrastructure or continuous internet connectivity. These solutions prioritise simplicity, offline functionality, and ease of use over the comprehensive
data management capabilities of enterprise-level electronic health record systems, making them more accessible and sustainable in low-resource deployment environments.
Thambawita et al. (2022) demonstrated the viability of this approach through the
Simple application, a user-centred offline-first digital health record system designed for
chronic disease management in low-resource settings. The application stored patient records locally on the device and synchronised with a central server when connectivity was
available, achieving a median patient registration time of 76 seconds and a median followup visit entry time of 13 seconds. The system scaled to reach more than 11,400 primary
care facilities in four countries with over three million patients enrolled, demonstrating
that a lightweight, offline-first mobile record system could achieve substantial coverage
in exactly the kind of low-resource environments that full-scale electronic health record
systems had failed to penetrate. The authors attributed this success to the explicit prioritisation of simplicity, offline functionality, and user-centred design informed by extensive
field testing with frontline health workers. Owotunde et al. (2021) developed and tested a
lightweight mobile physician assistant system for low-resource areas in Nigeria that combined electronic medical record functionality with AI-assisted diagnostic support, storing
patient data both locally on the device and in a cloud database, with encryption applied
before transmission to ensure data security. The authors reported that surveyed physicians
in Nigeria overwhelmingly used handwritten records at the time of the study, and that a
33

large majority expressed a preference for an Android-based application if one were made
available. This finding confirmed that demand for mobile-based patient record solutions
among healthcare workers in low-resource African settings was genuine and unmet by
existing tools.
The digital patient record component of the present study built directly on these foundations. Rather than attempting to deploy a full-scale electronic health record system, the
study adopted a lightweight mobile-based approach in which patient demographic information, medical history, diagnosis results, AI-generated predictions, and doctor notes
were stored in a structured local SQLite database on the device and synchronised with a
PostgreSQL database on the central Django server when internet connectivity was available. Patients accessed their own records through the mobile application in a simplified
view analogous to a digital consultation booklet, displaying their diagnosis history, doctor notes, and current health status in plain language without exposing technical model
outputs or AI confidence scores. This design was explicitly motivated by the evidence
reviewed in this section, which consistently identified simplicity, offline capability, and
user-centred design as the critical determinants of adoption success for mobile health record solutions in low-resource African healthcare settings.
2.10 Security in Mobile Health Applications
2.10.1 Overview of Mobile Health Application Security
The security of mobile health applications constitutes one of the most critical and
frequently underaddressed dimensions of mHealth system design. Mobile health applications collect, store, transmit, and process sensitive patient health information, including
demographic data, medical history, diagnostic results, and clinical notes, all of which
constitute Protected Health Information that carries significant legal, ethical, and clinical
consequences if exposed to unauthorised parties. Rezaee et al. (2023) conducted a comprehensive study to develop and validate a security assessment tool for mobile health
applications and found that many existing mHealth applications provided an insecure
infrastructure, with security consistently deprioritised by developers in favour of functionality and user experience. The study identified authentication weakness, inadequate
data encryption, and insufficient access control as the most prevalent security deficiencies
across reviewed mHealth applications.

34

The scale of the security risk in digital health is substantial and growing. Hariri et al.
(2025) documented that healthcare data breaches in 2023 alone involved 712 major incidents affecting over 87.2 million patient records, with an average cost of 429 United
States dollars per breached record, compared to 164 dollars across all other industries.
The authors reported that biometric authentication systems had demonstrated unauthorised access reductions of up to 68% compared to conventional password-based methods,
underscoring the clinical and operational case for robust authentication in healthcare mobile applications. For the present study, which handles patient diagnostic data and medical
records in a resource-constrained environment where devices may be shared or accessed
by multiple individuals, robust security design was not an optional enhancement but a
fundamental design requirement.
The security architecture of the present study was therefore designed around two distinct and complementary security layers. The first layer, implemented at the application
level through JWT-based authentication and role-based access control, governs which
users are permitted to access the system and what data and functions they are authorised
to use. The second layer, implemented at the device level through biometric and PIN
authentication, governs physical access to the application on the device itself, ensuring
that unauthorised individuals cannot access the system even if they gain possession of the
device.
2.10.2 Authentication and Authorisation Frameworks
Authentication refers to the process of verifying the identity of a user attempting to
access a system, whilst authorisation refers to the process of determining what resources
and functions that verified user is permitted to access. In the context of mobile health
applications, both processes must be implemented robustly to protect patient data from
unauthorised access whilst remaining sufficiently usable to avoid impeding clinical workflows under time pressure. Alhammad et al. (2024) conducted a systematic review of patients’ perspectives on data confidentiality, privacy, and security in mHealth applications
and found that authentication concerns were the most frequently cited security issue, with
patients expressing particular concern about the risk of unauthorised access to their health
information by third parties, including other household members sharing the same device.
Modern mobile health applications employ a range of authentication mechanisms
ranging from simple username and password combinations to multi-factor authentica35

tion schemes combining knowledge factors, possession factors, and biometric factors.
The consensus in recent security literature is that username and password authentication
alone is insufficient for applications handling sensitive health data, and that at minimum
a second authentication factor should be implemented to reduce the risk of credential
compromise Aljedaani et al. (2021). The present study implemented a two-factor authentication architecture comprising application-level JWT-based credential verification as the
first factor and device-level biometric or PIN verification as the second factor, providing
layered protection against both remote credential attacks and physical device access by
unauthorised individuals.
2.10.3 JSON Web Token Authentication
JSON Web Token, standardised as RFC 7519, is an open standard for securely transmitting information between two parties as a compact, URL-safe, digitally signed JSON
object. A JWT consists of three components: a header specifying the token type and cryptographic signing algorithm, a payload containing claims about the authenticated user including their identity and role, and a cryptographic signature generated by combining the
encoded header and payload with a secret key held by the server Syofian et al. (2024). The
signature ensures that the token cannot be tampered with in transit, as any modification to
the header or payload would invalidate the signature when verified by the server.
JWT-based authentication is stateless, meaning that the server does not maintain a
session store or database lookup table for authenticated users. Instead, the client application stores the JWT after successful login and includes it in the authorisation header of
every subsequent API request. The server verifies the JWT signature on each request and
extracts the user identity and role from the payload without requiring any database query,
making JWT authentication particularly well-suited to mobile applications that communicate with a backend server via REST API Syofian et al. (2024). This stateless design
reduces server-side complexity, improves scalability, and is compatible with offline-first
architectures in which the client application must continue to function without continuous
server communication.
In the present study, JWT authentication was implemented between the Flutter mobile
application and the Django REST backend. Upon successful login with valid credentials,
the backend issued a signed JWT containing the user’s identity, role (doctor or patient),
and token expiry timestamp. This token was stored securely on the mobile device and in36

cluded in the authorisation header of all subsequent API requests. Expired tokens required
re-authentication, limiting the window of risk in the event of token compromise. This approach is consistent with industry best practices for securing REST API communication
in mobile health applications.
2.10.4 Role-Based Access Control
Role-Based Access Control is an access control model in which permissions to access system resources and functions are assigned to predefined roles rather than to individual users, and users are granted permissions by being assigned to one or more roles.
Sandhu et al. Sandhu et al. (1996) introduced the foundational RBAC model, which has
since become one of the most widely adopted access control paradigms in enterprise and
healthcare information systems. In a healthcare context, RBAC enables the enforcement
of the principle of least privilege, whereby each user is granted access only to the data and
functions necessary for their specific clinical or administrative role, minimising the risk of
inadvertent or malicious access to sensitive patient information beyond what is required
for legitimate clinical purposes.
Implementing Role-Based Access Control for healthcare applications has been identified as a critical security best practice in the literature, particularly for systems that serve
multiple user types with fundamentally different data access requirements Rathi et al.
(2025). In the present study, two primary roles were defined: the doctor role and the
patient role. Users assigned the doctor role were granted access to all patient records
registered within the system, the AI-assisted diagnostic interface, the AI clinical chat feature, and the ability to add diagnostic notes to patient records. Users assigned the patient
role were granted access exclusively to their own health record, including their personal
information, diagnosis history, and doctor notes, without access to any other patient’s data,
the diagnostic AI interface, or the AI chat. This strict role separation ensured that patient
data privacy was maintained by design rather than by policy alone, making unauthorised
cross-patient data access architecturally impossible regardless of user behaviour.
2.10.5 Biometric and PIN-Based Device Security
Whilst application-level authentication controls access to the system through credential verification, a complementary layer of device-level security is necessary to protect
patient data in the event that an authenticated user’s mobile device is physically accessed
37

by an unauthorised individual. In clinical environments, where a doctor’s device may be
set down during a consultation or a patient’s phone may be used by a family member,
the risk of unauthorised device access is real and practically significant. This risk is particularly relevant in the healthcare settings targeted by the present study, where devices
are frequently carried by clinicians between multiple patient consultations throughout the
day.
Biometric authentication, encompassing fingerprint recognition and facial recognition, provides a highly usable and highly secure mechanism for device-level access control
in mobile health applications. Hariri et al. (2025) reported that fingerprint recognition was
the most widely adopted biometric modality in healthcare applications, used in 62% of
healthcare biometric implementations, followed by facial recognition at 19%. The authors
demonstrated that biometric authentication systems reduced unauthorised access rates by
up to 68% compared to password-based systems, whilst simultaneously improving user
experience by eliminating the need to enter passwords at each access event. Aljedaani et
al. Aljedaani et al. (2021) further noted in their empirical study of mHealth user security
preferences that users strongly desired biometric authentication and perceived it as positively impacting security without significantly compromising usability, a critical balance
in time-constrained clinical environments.
In the present study, device-level biometric and PIN authentication was implemented using the local authentication framework available natively in Flutter through the
local_auth package, which provides access to the operating system’s built-in fingerprint
and face recognition APIs on both Android and iOS devices without requiring the transmission of any biometric data to the server or to external services. After the initial
application-level JWT login, the application required biometric or PIN verification each
time it was opened or brought from the background, ensuring that only the authenticated
device owner could access the application in normal use. This implementation maintained
full biometric processing on-device, preserving patient privacy by ensuring that biometric
data never left the user’s device.

2.11 Software Development Life Cycle Models
2.11.1 Overview of Software Development Life Cycle
The Software Development Life Cycle refers to a structured process that defines the
phases, activities, and tasks involved in planning, designing, developing, testing, deploying, and maintaining a software system. The primary purpose of the SDLC is to minimise
the risk of project failure and maximise the quality of the delivered software product by
providing a systematic and repeatable framework that guides the development team from
the initial definition of requirements through to the final delivery and maintenance of the
system Gurung et al. (2020). Without a structured SDLC framework, software development is prone to cost overruns, schedule slippage, scope creep, and the delivery of systems
that do not meet user requirements or quality standards.
Numerous SDLC models have been proposed in the software engineering literature,
each offering a different approach to organising and sequencing the activities of the development process. The most widely reviewed models in academic and professional contexts
include the Waterfall model, the Spiral model, the Iterative model, and the Agile methodology. Each model has distinct characteristics, strengths, and limitations that determine its
suitability for different project types, team sizes, and operating conditions. The following
subsections present a review of each of these models before justifying the selection of the
Agile methodology for the present study.
2.11.2 Waterfall Model
The Waterfall model is the earliest and most widely recognised formalised SDLC
model, characterised by a strictly sequential linear flow of development activities in which
each phase must be completed in full before the subsequent phase begins. The standard
phases of the Waterfall model are requirements gathering, system design, implementation,
testing, deployment, and maintenance, each of which produces a defined set of deliverables that serve as the input to the next phase Sommerville (2016). The model takes its
name from the unidirectional flow of progress from one phase to the next, analogous to
water flowing downward over a series of steps.
The principal advantage of the Waterfall model lies in its simplicity and clarity. Because all requirements are defined and documented at the outset of the project, the development timeline and resource requirements can be estimated with relative precision,

39

and the sequential structure makes it straightforward to track progress and assign responsibilities across the development team. The model is well-suited to projects with clearly
defined, stable, and comprehensively understood requirements that are unlikely to change
substantially during development Gurung et al. (2020).
However, the Waterfall model imposes a fundamental limitation in the form of its inflexibility in the face of changing requirements. Because the requirements phase precedes
all subsequent phases and the model does not accommodate revisiting earlier phases once
they are complete, any change to requirements discovered during implementation or testing necessitates restarting the development process from an earlier stage, incurring significant cost and schedule penalties. This rigidity makes the Waterfall model poorly suited
to projects characterised by evolving requirements, uncertain technical feasibility, or the
need for iterative refinement based on user feedback, all of which are characteristic of AIbased healthcare software development. Furthermore, the deferral of all testing activities
to the post-implementation phase means that defects and design flaws are discovered late
in the development cycle when they are most costly to rectify.
2.11.3 Spiral Model
The Spiral model was introduced by Boehm (1988) as a risk-driven alternative to the
linear sequential approach of the Waterfall model. The model represents the software development process as a series of iterative cycles, or spirals, each of which passes through
four quadrants: objective setting, risk assessment and reduction, development and validation, and planning for the next cycle. Each completed spiral represents one phase of
the overall development process, with the product progressively elaborated and refined
through successive iterations.
The defining characteristic of the Spiral model is its explicit emphasis on risk identification and mitigation at each iteration. Before proceeding to development in each spiral,
the team conducts a structured risk assessment to identify the most significant technical,
schedule, and requirement risks facing the project, and takes deliberate steps to reduce
those risks before committing to further development. This risk-driven approach makes
the Spiral model particularly appropriate for large, complex, high-stakes software projects
where the cost of late-stage failure is severe Boehm (1988).
The primary limitations of the Spiral model are its complexity and the high level
of risk management expertise required to apply it effectively. The model demands that
40

the development team be proficient in formal risk identification and quantification techniques, which may not be readily available in all development contexts. Furthermore, the
overhead associated with the risk assessment activities at each spiral can be substantial,
making the model poorly suited to smaller projects or teams with limited resources and
time. For the present study, which was conducted by a single researcher within a constrained time frame, the full rigour of the Spiral model’s risk management process was
not practically feasible.
2.11.4 Iterative Model
The Iterative model, also referred to as the Iterative and Incremental model, addresses
the inflexibility of the Waterfall model by decomposing the overall development process
into a series of short development cycles, each of which produces a working increment
of the software system. Development begins with the implementation of a partial set of
requirements, and each subsequent iteration adds functionality, refines existing features,
or addresses defects identified during the previous cycle, progressively building toward
the complete system Sommerville (2016).
The Iterative model offers several advantages over the Waterfall model for projects
with evolving or incompletely understood requirements. By delivering working software increments at regular intervals, the model enables early detection of requirement
misunderstandings, design flaws, and technical feasibility problems, all of which can be
addressed in subsequent iterations before they accumulate into costly late-stage failures.
User and stakeholder feedback can be incorporated between iterations, ensuring that the
delivered system progressively aligns more closely with actual user needs. However, the
Iterative model does not provide an explicit framework for managing the overall project
scope, team collaboration, or the prioritisation of requirements across iterations, which
can lead to scope creep and uncontrolled expansion of the project if not carefully managed.
2.11.5 Agile Methodology
The Agile methodology is a family of iterative and incremental software development
approaches united by the values and principles articulated in the Agile Manifesto, published by Beck et al. (2001). The Manifesto prioritises individuals and interactions over
processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer col41

laboration over contract negotiation, and responding to change over following a plan.
These values represent a fundamental departure from the document-driven, plan-driven
philosophy of traditional SDLC models such as the Waterfall model, emphasising adaptability, continuous delivery, and active stakeholder engagement as the primary drivers of
successful software development.
In practice, Agile development is organised around short, time-boxed development
cycles called sprints, typically lasting between one and four weeks. At the beginning of
each sprint, the development team selects a prioritised set of features or user stories from
the product backlog and commits to delivering a working, tested increment of the system
by the end of the sprint. Each sprint concludes with a review in which the delivered
increment is demonstrated to stakeholders and a retrospective in which the team reflects
on and improves its development process. This cycle repeats until the product backlog is
exhausted or the system meets the required acceptance criteria.
The application of Agile methodology to healthcare software development has been
the subject of growing research interest. Kokol et al. (2022) conducted a synthetic scoping review of Agile software development in healthcare and found that Agile adoption in
healthcare software projects had grown exponentially over the reviewed period, with citation counts increasing from 13 in 2008 to 394 in 2022, reflecting the broad recognition of
Agile’s suitability for the complex, evolving, and user-centred requirements of healthcare
system development. The review identified four primary research themes in the literature:
the adaptation of Agile practices to regulatory compliance requirements, the integration
of user-centred design within Agile workflows, the application of Agile to mobile health
application development specifically, and the use of Agile for the deployment of machine
learning models in clinical settings.
The specific application of Agile methodology to machine learning system development in healthcare was addressed by Gaines et al. Gaines et al. (2021), who demonstrated
that Agile development practices were well-suited to the iterative nature of machine learning model training, evaluation, and refinement. The authors described how sprint-based
development enabled the team to progressively improve model performance through successive iterations of data preprocessing, architecture selection, hyperparameter tuning,
and evaluation, with each sprint delivering a measurable improvement in model metrics.
This iterative approach is directly analogous to the model development process followed

42

in the present study, where each CNN training run produced measurable outcomes that
informed subsequent decisions about augmentation strategies, learning rates, and model
architecture modifications.
2.11.6 Justification for Agile in the Present Study
The selection of the Agile methodology as the SDLC framework for the present study
was informed by the specific characteristics of the project and the limitations of the alternative models reviewed above.
The Waterfall model was considered and rejected on the grounds that the requirements
of an AI-based mobile healthcare system cannot be comprehensively and definitively specified at the outset of the project. The performance of the CNN models for tuberculosis
and malaria detection, the specific augmentation parameters required for robustness to degraded images, the optimal architecture configuration for on-device deployment, and the
precise data structures required for the digital patient record system were all subject to
empirical discovery through iterative experimentation during the development process. A
sequential model that required all requirements to be fixed before implementation began
would have been fundamentally incompatible with this research-driven development context.
The Spiral model was considered and rejected on practical grounds. Whilst its riskdriven approach has conceptual merit for a project involving novel AI deployment in a
clinical context, the formal risk quantification and management overhead of the Spiral
model was not feasible for a single-researcher Master’s dissertation project conducted
within a constrained time frame.
The Agile methodology was selected because it accommodated the iterative and empirical nature of the project across all of its components. For the AI model development component, successive sprints enabled the progressive refinement of preprocessing
pipelines, augmentation strategies, and model architectures based on measured performance outcomes. For the mobile application development component, iterative sprint cycles
enabled the progressive delivery and testing of individual system features, including authentication, patient record management, AI diagnostic integration, and the AI chat interface, with each sprint producing a testable increment of the application. For the system
integration component, Agile’s emphasis on continuous integration and early testing enabled compatibility issues between the mobile application, backend API, and AI models
43

to be identified and resolved progressively rather than at the end of the development cycle.
Furthermore, Odera et al. (2023) demonstrated that the Agile methodology offered
significant advantages specifically for AI-enabled mobile health applications, including
the ability to incorporate ethical considerations iteratively throughout the development
lifecycle rather than as a retrospective activity, and the facilitation of community and
clinician involvement in the design process through regular sprint reviews. These advantages were particularly relevant to the present study, where the clinical safety and ethical
appropriateness of the AI diagnostic tool were ongoing design considerations throughout
the development process rather than post-hoc compliance activities.
2.12 Existing AI-Based Healthcare Systems
2.12.1 Ada Health
Ada Health is a mobile AI-powered symptom assessment application founded in Berlin in 2011 and currently available in over 130 countries. The application uses a large
medical knowledge base and a probabilistic inference engine to evaluate user-reported
symptoms and generate a ranked list of possible conditions, along with recommendations
for the appropriate level of care to seek Khanna et al. (2024). A peer-reviewed study published in BMJ Open demonstrated that Ada Health achieved accuracy levels approaching
those of human general practitioners in standardised clinical vignette testing, outperforming most other commercially available symptom checkers Gilbert et al. (2020).
However, Ada Health presents several critical limitations relative to the requirements
of the present study. The system relies exclusively on patient-reported symptoms and
does not incorporate medical imaging, laboratory data, or any form of clinical diagnostic
analysis. The application is designed for patient self-assessment rather than clinical decision support, and therefore does not address the needs of healthcare providers requiring AI-assisted interpretation of diagnostic images. Furthermore, Ada Health requires
continuous internet connectivity to function, has no offline capability, does not maintain
longitudinal patient records accessible to healthcare providers, and provides no facility
for doctor-patient communication or AI-assisted clinical chat. These limitations collectively render Ada Health unsuitable as a model for deployment in low-resource peripheral
healthcare settings in Cameroon.

44

2.12.2 Babylon Health
Babylon Health was a United Kingdom-based AI health service founded in 2013 that
combined symptom checking, telemedicine consultations, and AI-assisted clinical decision support within a single platform. The company’s AI system achieved notable performance on standardised diagnostic benchmarks, with a peer-reviewed study published
in Nature Communications demonstrating that Babylon’s causal machine learning approach outperformed 72% of general practitioners on written diagnostic test cases Richens
et al. (2020). The platform was deployed in Rwanda as Babyl in partnership with the Bill
and Melinda Gates Foundation, reaching over two million members by 2018.
Despite these achievements, Babylon Health encountered critical limitations in its
African deployment. At the time of its Rwanda launch, the Babylon AI system did not include tuberculosis or malaria among the conditions it could diagnose, representing a fundamental gap relative to the disease burden of the deployment context Wikipedia (2024).
The platform required stable internet connectivity and was not designed for offline operation. Babylon Health entered bankruptcy in 2023 and ceased operations, underscoring
the sustainability challenges facing AI health platforms that are not designed with the
operational realities of low-resource environments as a primary consideration.
2.12.3 Waspito
Waspito is a Cameroonian digital health platform founded in 2020 that provides instant video teleconsultation between patients and verified doctors, mobile laboratory sample
collection, pharmacy services, and a health-focused social network. By 2023, the platform
had reached over 650,000 users in Cameroon and Ivory Coast, onboarded 950 doctors, and
facilitated over 60,000 consultations, raising a total of 5.2 million United States dollars
in funding People of Color in Tech (2023). Waspito represents the most advanced locally developed digital health initiative in Cameroon and demonstrates the feasibility and
demand for mobile health services in the Cameroonian context.
However, Waspito does not incorporate AI-based diagnostic capabilities of any kind.
The platform facilitates human doctor consultations remotely but does not provide AIassisted analysis of medical images, automated disease detection, or clinical decision
support for healthcare providers working in facilities without specialist access. The platform also requires reliable internet connectivity for its core teleconsultation functionality

45

and does not maintain structured longitudinal patient records accessible to healthcare providers across different facilities. These limitations mean that Waspito does not address the
diagnostic support needs of district hospitals and health centres in the North-West Region
of Cameroon where connectivity is unreliable and specialist teleconsultation may not be
feasible.
2.12.4 Research-Based AI Diagnostic Tools
Beyond commercial platforms, a substantial body of research has demonstrated the
technical feasibility of deep learning for infectious disease diagnosis specifically relevant to the Cameroonian context. Deep learning models for tuberculosis detection from
chest X-rays, as reviewed in Section 2.4, have achieved near-expert diagnostic performance on benchmark datasets. Similarly, CNN-based models for malaria detection from
blood smear images, as reviewed in Section 2.5, have demonstrated high accuracy under
controlled conditions. However, these research-based systems share a consistent set of
limitations: they address single diseases in isolation, they have been validated exclusively
on high-quality benchmark datasets without accounting for real-world image degradation,
they have not been integrated into mobile platforms with offline capability, and they have
not been combined with patient record management or clinical decision support features
within a unified system Santosh et al. (2022); Yusuf et al. (2024).

2.13 Research Gap
The review of existing literature presented in this chapter reveals that whilst significant
progress has been made in the application of artificial intelligence to healthcare delivery,
medical image analysis, mobile health systems, and digital patient record management,
a substantial and clearly defined gap exists between the current state of knowledge and
the practical requirements of AI-assisted clinical diagnosis and patient management in
low-resource healthcare settings such as Cameroon.
The first identified gap concerns the domain specificity and robustness of existing AI
diagnostic models. As demonstrated in Sections 2.4 and 2.5, convolutional neural network
models for tuberculosis detection from chest X-rays and malaria detection from blood
smear images have achieved high diagnostic accuracy under controlled benchmark conditions. However, Santosh et al. (2022) and Yusuf et al. (2024) independently identified that
the overwhelming majority of published models were trained and evaluated exclusively
on high-quality datasets collected under controlled imaging conditions and had not been
evaluated for robustness to the image degradation and domain shift that characterise real-

47

world clinical imaging in low-resource settings. Sendra-Balcells et al. (2023) confirmed
that this generalisation failure was a documented and persistent problem for AI medical
imaging systems deployed in African healthcare contexts. No existing study has systematically evaluated the combined effect of domain-shift-aware data augmentation, MixUp
training, and transfer learning as an integrated robustness pipeline for both tuberculosis
and malaria detection models intended for deployment in peripheral health facilities in
Cameroon.
The second identified gap concerns the mobile optimisation of medical AI models for
low-connectivity African healthcare environments. As reviewed in Section 2.7, whilst
model quantisation and TorchScript-based mobile deployment have been demonstrated
for individual diagnostic tasks, no existing study has developed and evaluated a complete
mobile optimisation pipeline for both a tuberculosis detection model and a malaria detection model within a single hybrid offline and online application architecture. Kirefu et al.
(2021) identified the absence of offline functionality as the most consistently reported barrier to mHealth adoption in sub-Saharan Africa, yet the majority of existing AI-assisted
diagnostic tools reviewed in Section 2.12 require continuous internet connectivity for their
core functionality, rendering them fundamentally unsuitable for deployment in the NorthWest Region of Cameroon where connectivity is unreliable.
The third identified gap concerns the integration of AI-assisted diagnosis with digital
patient record management and clinical decision support within a single unified mobile
platform. As reviewed in Section 2.12, existing systems address these requirements in
isolation. Ada Health provides symptom-based assessment without imaging capability,
offline support, or patient records. Babylon Health combined AI with telemedicine but
lacked image-based diagnosis, offline capability, and did not address the specific diseases of highest burden in Cameroon. Research-based AI diagnostic tools address single
diseases without integration into broader clinical workflows. Waspito provides teleconsultation and limited patient connectivity but incorporates no AI diagnostic capability.
No existing system integrates AI-assisted medical image diagnosis for both malaria and
tuberculosis, longitudinal digital patient records accessible to both doctors and patients,
a patient-aware AI clinical chat feature for clinical decision support, and a hybrid offline
and online architecture within a single secure mobile application.
The fourth identified gap concerns the security architecture of mobile health applic-

48

ations in low-resource settings. As reviewed in Section 2.10, whilst the importance of
robust authentication and access control in mHealth applications is well-established in
the literature, and biometric authentication has been demonstrated to substantially reduce
unauthorised access rates in clinical settings, the majority of mHealth systems reviewed
in Section 2.12 do not implement two-layer security combining application-level JWT
authentication with device-level biometric verification. This is a particularly significant
omission in the context of applications that handle sensitive patient diagnostic data in environments where devices may be shared or physically accessed by multiple individuals.
The fifth identified gap concerns the contextual appropriateness of existing AI health
systems for Cameroonian healthcare delivery specifically. As reviewed in Sections 2.1
and 2.8, AI systems designed for high-resource environments consistently underperform
in low-resource deployment contexts due to data quality variability, infrastructure constraints, and the absence of locally representative training data. The Anglophone crisis
documented in Section 1.1 has further reduced healthcare access in the North-West Region, creating an acute and specific need for a system that functions reliably under conditions of degraded connectivity, limited equipment quality, and reduced specialist availability. No existing system has been specifically designed to address the combined healthcare
delivery challenges of the Cameroonian peripheral health system.
Based on these five identified gaps, the present study addressed a clearly defined and
practically significant research problem by designing and developing a mobile artificial
intelligence system that integrated robust CNN-based diagnosis for malaria and tuberculosis, domain-shift-aware model training for real-world deployment conditions, mobile
model optimisation for hybrid offline and online inference, digital patient record management, patient-aware AI clinical chat support, and a two-layer security architecture, within
a single unified application tailored to the operational realities of low-resource healthcare
delivery in Cameroon. Table 2.2 below provides a structured mapping of the identified

MATERIALS AND METHODS
3.1 Introduction
This chapter presents the materials and methods employed in the design and development of a mobile artificial intelligence system for clinical diagnosis support and patient
management in low-resource healthcare settings. The chapter is organised to correspond
directly with the four specific objectives of the study. It begins with an overview of
the proposed system and the software development methodology adopted, followed by
a description of the system requirements, system design, dataset preparation, AI model
development, mobile optimisation strategy, system implementation, and evaluation methodology. All design decisions presented in this chapter were informed by the findings of
the literature review presented in Chapter 2 and were guided by the operational realities
of peripheral healthcare delivery in the North-West Region of Cameroon.
3.2 System Overview
The proposed system is a mobile-based artificial intelligence platform designed to
support clinical diagnosis and patient management in low-resource healthcare settings.
The system integrates two deep learning models for disease detection, a digital patient
record component, and a patient-aware AI clinical chat feature within a single secure
mobile application deployable on Android and iOS devices.

3.6 Dataset Description and Preparation
3.6.1 Tuberculosis Dataset Description
Two publicly available chest X-ray datasets were used for the training and evaluation
of the tuberculosis detection model. The first dataset, the Shenzhen Chest X-ray Dataset, was collected from Shenzhen No. 3 People’s Hospital in collaboration with Guangdong Medical College in China and contains 662 posterior-anterior chest X-ray images, of
which 336 are tuberculosis positive and 326 are normal Jaeger et al. (2014). The second
dataset, the Montgomery County Chest X-ray Dataset, was collected from the Department of Health and Human Services of Montgomery County, Maryland, and contains
138 posterior-anterior chest X-ray images, of which 58 are tuberculosis positive and 80
are normal Jaeger et al. (2014). Both datasets were collected under real-world clinical

65

conditions and contain associated radiologist readings confirming the ground truth labels.
The two datasets were combined to produce a unified tuberculosis dataset of 800 images,
comprising 394 tuberculosis positive and 406 normal cases, providing a near-balanced
class distribution suitable for binary classification training.
3.6.2 Malaria Dataset Description
The NIH Malaria Cell Images Dataset was used for the training and evaluation of
the malaria detection model. The dataset was developed by researchers at the Lister Hill
National Center for Biomedical Communications at the United States National Library
of Medicine and contains 27,558 segmented cell images derived from Giemsa-stained
thin blood smear slides prepared from 200 patients Rajaraman et al. (2018). The dataset
comprises 13,779 parasitised cell images and 13,779 uninfected cell images, providing
a perfectly balanced binary classification dataset. A critically important characteristic of
this dataset for the present study is that the original slide images were captured using a
smartphone camera held to the eyepiece of a light microscope, closely mirroring the image acquisition conditions anticipated in the target deployment environment of peripheral
health facilities in Cameroon.
3.6.3 Data Preprocessing for Tuberculosis Images
All chest X-ray images were preprocessed prior to training to ensure consistency and
compatibility with the MobileNetV2 input requirements. Preprocessing steps applied to
tuberculosis images included resizing all images to 224 by 224 pixels, converting all images to RGB format, and applying ImageNet mean and standard deviation normalisation
with mean values of 0.485, 0.456, and 0.406 and standard deviation values of 0.229,
0.224, and 0.225 across the three colour channels respectively. These normalisation values are standard for ImageNet-pretrained models and ensure that the input distribution
of the fine-tuning data is consistent with that used during the original pre-training of the
MobileNetV2 backbone.
3.6.4 Data Preprocessing for Malaria Images
Malaria cell images were preprocessed using the same resizing and normalisation
pipeline applied to tuberculosis images. Additionally, given the relatively small and uniform size of the segmented cell images in the NIH dataset, a centre crop operation was

66

applied prior to resizing to remove peripheral artefacts introduced during the automated
cell segmentation process. This step improved the consistency of the cell region presented
to the model across the dataset.

3.7 Model Design and Development
3.7.1 Selection of MobileNetV2 Architecture
The MobileNetV2 architecture was selected as the backbone for both the tuberculosis and malaria detection models based on three principal criteria. First, MobileNetV2 was specifically designed for efficient inference on mobile and resource-constrained
devices, achieving competitive classification accuracy whilst requiring substantially fewer
parameters and multiply-add operations than larger architectures such as ResNet50 and
VGG16 Sandler et al. (2018). Second, the inverted residual and linear bottleneck design
of MobileNetV2 has been demonstrated to quantise with minimal accuracy degradation,
making it particularly well-suited to the post-training quantisation pipeline required for
on-device deployment in the present study. Third, MobileNetV2 is available with weights
pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset through the PyTorch torchvision library, enabling
immediate application of transfer learning without requiring additional data collection or
67

computational resources for pre-training.
In addition to quantisation, structured pruning was considered as a complementary
model compression strategy to further reduce the computational footprint of the deployed
models. Structured pruning removes entire convolutional filters that contribute least to
the model output, producing a genuinely smaller and faster network rather than simply
reducing numerical precision. However, the application of structured pruning requires
sufficient training data to fine-tune the pruned model and recover accuracy lost during
the pruning process. Given that the malaria detection model was trained on 27,558 images, sufficient data was available to support pruning at a conservative rate of 20% of
filters followed by fine-tuning. In contrast, the tuberculosis detection model was trained
on a combined dataset of only 800 images, which was considered insufficient to reliably recover diagnostic accuracy after pruning. Structured pruning was therefore applied
exclusively to the malaria detection model, whilst the tuberculosis detection model was
compressed through quantisation alone. This decision reflects the principle that model
compression strategies must be selected in accordance with the specific data availability constraints of each task, a consideration that is particularly important in low-resource
medical AI deployment contexts Deng et al. (2020).
3.7.2 Transfer Learning Strategy
Transfer learning was applied to both models by initialising the MobileNetV2 backbone with ImageNet pre-trained weights and replacing the original classification head
with a custom binary classification head consisting of a dropout layer with a rate of 0.3,
a fully connected layer with 256 units and ReLU activation, a second dropout layer with
a rate of 0.2, and a final fully connected layer with a single output unit followed by a
sigmoid activation function producing a probability score between zero and one. The sigmoid output represents the probability of the positive class, with tuberculosis positive or
malaria parasitised respectively.
During the initial training phase, the early layers of the MobileNetV2 backbone were
frozen to preserve the low-level and mid-level feature representations learned during ImageNet pre-training. Only the custom classification head and the final twenty layers of the
backbone were left trainable. This selective fine-tuning strategy reduced the risk of overfitting on the relatively small tuberculosis dataset whilst allowing the higher-level feature
representations of the backbone to be adapted to the specific visual characteristics of chest
68

X-ray and blood smear images. In the second training phase, all layers were unfrozen and
the entire network was fine-tuned at a reduced learning rate to allow global optimisation
of the model parameters.
3.7.3 Domain-Shift-Aware Augmentation Strategy
A domain-shift-aware data augmentation pipeline was designed for each model to
improve robustness to the image quality variability expected in the target deployment
environment. The augmentation strategy was informed by the specific sources of image
degradation anticipated in peripheral health facilities in Cameroon, as reviewed in Section
2.6.
For the tuberculosis detection model, augmentation transforms simulating real-world
chest X-ray variability were applied during training. These included random rotation
within a range of plus or minus fifteen degrees to simulate variable patient positioning,
random horizontal flipping, brightness and contrast variation within a range of plus or
minus 30% to simulate variable X-ray exposure settings, Gaussian blur with a kernel size
of three to simulate low-resolution digital X-ray equipment, and random affine transformation with a translation range of ten percent to simulate patient movement during image
acquisition.
For the malaria detection model, additional augmentation transforms were applied to
simulate the specific degradation introduced by smartphone-captured microscopy images.
These included vignetting simulation achieved by applying a radially varying darkening
mask to the image to replicate the circular aperture effect of the microscope eyepiece,
colour jitter with hue and saturation variation to simulate the automatic white balance
correction of smartphone cameras, and defocus blur simulation to replicate variable focus
resulting from manual camera positioning. The complete augmentation parameters for
both models are presented in Table 3.3.

3.7.4 MixUp Training
MixUp training was applied to both the tuberculosis and malaria detection models
during the training phase to improve model robustness to ambiguous and degraded input
images. As described in Section 2.6, MixUp generates virtual training examples by computing convex combinations of pairs of training images and their corresponding labels,
training the model to produce smooth and well-calibrated output probability distributions
rather than sharp memorised decision boundaries Zhang et al. (2018). An alpha value of
0.2 was used for the Beta distribution from which the mixing coefficient was sampled,
consistent with the value recommended in the original MixUp publication and widely
adopted in subsequent medical imaging studies. This technique was particularly valuable
for the tuberculosis detection model, where the small size of the training dataset increased
the risk of overfitting to specific image characteristics present in the training set.

to its adaptive learning rate properties and demonstrated effectiveness for fine-tuning pretrained convolutional neural network models on small to medium sized medical imaging
datasets. An early stopping strategy was applied during training to prevent overfitting,
whereby training was halted if the validation loss did not improve for five consecutive
epochs. The model weights corresponding to the best validation loss observed during
training were retained as the final model parameters. This strategy was particularly important for the tuberculosis model given the small size of the combined training dataset.

3.8 Mobile Model Optimisation and Deployment
3.8.1 Overview of the Compression Pipeline
The deployment of deep learning models on mobile devices in low-resource environments requires a systematic model compression pipeline that reduces model size and
inference latency whilst preserving diagnostic accuracy to a clinically acceptable level.
For the present study, a two-stage compression pipeline was designed and applied to both
the tuberculosis and malaria detection models following the completion of training. The
pipeline comprised structured filter pruning followed by post-training dynamic quantisation, with TorchScript export and PyTorch Mobile optimisation as the final deployment
steps. As discussed in Section 3.7.1, structured pruning was applied only to the malaria
detection model due to dataset size constraints, whilst quantisation was applied to both
models. The complete compression and deployment pipeline is illustrated in Figure 3.15.

74

Figure 3.8: Model Compression and Mobile Deployment Pipeline
As shown in Figure 3.8, the pipeline begins with the fully trained float32 model
and proceeds through pruning where applicable, fine-tuning after pruning, quantisation,

75

TorchScript conversion, and mobile optimisation before producing the final on-device
model file for integration into the Flutter mobile application.
3.8.2 Structured Filter Pruning (Malaria Model)
Structured pruning was applied to the malaria detection model using L1-norm filter
pruning, in which convolutional filters with the smallest L1 norm values were identified
and removed from each convolutional layer of the MobileNetV2 backbone. A global
pruning rate of 20% was applied, meaning that 20% of filters across all convolutional layers were removed. This rate was selected based on evidence from the model compression
literature demonstrating that pruning rates of 20 to 30% applied to MobileNet-family architectures achieve meaningful size reduction with minimal accuracy degradation when
followed by adequate fine-tuning Deng et al. (2020). Following pruning, the model was
fine-tuned for 10 additional epochs at a reduced learning rate of 0.00005 to allow the
remaining weights to adapt and recover accuracy lost during the pruning process.
3.8.3 Post-Training Dynamic Quantisation
Post-training dynamic quantisation was applied to both models following the completion of training and, for the malaria model, after pruning and fine-tuning. Dynamic quantisation converts the float32 weight tensors of the model to int8 integer representations,
reducing model size by approximately 75% and accelerating inference by exploiting the
integer arithmetic capabilities of mobile processors Wu et al. (2020). Dynamic quantisation was selected over static quantisation because it does not require a calibration dataset
to compute activation quantisation parameters, making it simpler to apply and more robust to distribution shift between training data and real-world deployment images. This
property is particularly important in the low-resource deployment context of the present
study, where clinical images may differ statistically from the training dataset.
3.8.4 TorchScript Conversion and Mobile Optimisation
Following quantisation, both models were exported to the TorchScript format using
the torch.jit.trace function, which traces the model execution graph using a representative
dummy input tensor and produces a self-contained platform-independent model representation executable without a Python runtime PyTorch Foundation (2023). The traced
TorchScript model was subsequently optimised for mobile inference using the optim-

76

ize_for_mobile utility provided by the PyTorch Mobile framework, applying operator
fusion, memory layout optimisation, and removal of training-specific operations unnecessary for inference. The optimised model was saved in the PyTorch Lite Interpreter format
with the .ptl file extension, which is the format natively supported by the PyTorch Mobile
runtime embedded within the Flutter application.
3.8.5 Hybrid Offline and Online Inference Architecture
The compressed on-device models were integrated within the Flutter mobile application alongside a connectivity-aware inference routing mechanism that automatically selects between on-device and server-based inference depending on network availability.
When internet connectivity is detected, diagnostic image requests are routed to the Django
backend server where inference is performed using the full-precision float32 models.
When connectivity is unavailable, inference is performed locally using the compressed
on-device models, ensuring diagnostic support remains continuously available regardless
of network status.
Each diagnostic result includes a source flag field explicitly indicating whether the result was produced by the full-precision server model or the compressed on-device model.
This flag is displayed prominently in the result interface so that clinicians are always
aware of the inference context. Results generated in offline mode are stored in the local
SQLite database with a synchronisation status of pending and are automatically transmitted to the PostgreSQL server database when connectivity is restored.
